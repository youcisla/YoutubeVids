## EPISODE A1: "The person you're comparing yourself to doesn't exist"

### Layer 1 — Hook
Title (60 chars): The person you're comparing yourself to doesn't exist
Opening 15s: "The person you're comparing yourself to right now... doesn't exist. You're comparing your behind-the-scenes to their highlight reel. But it gets worse — your brain actually knows this and does it anyway."
Implicit promise: You'll learn why comparison is a cognitive distortion and how to stop it
Retention risk: Viewers who feel attacked might click off — balance with empathy

### Layer 2 — Script
**Structure:** 7-9 min video (for initial testing, we'll do a 60-90s Short version first)

**Short version (60-90s):**

[Hook - 0:00]
The person you're comparing yourself to right now... doesn't exist.

[Stakes - 0:08]
You're comparing your worst day — the morning you spilled coffee on your notes and nothing worked — to their best second. A 0.6-second clip they recorded twelve times until it looked effortless.

[Open loop - 0:20]
But here's the part nobody tells you. Social comparison isn't just bad for your mood. It actively changes how your brain processes success.

[Content - 0:30]
When you compare upward, your brain releases cortisol. Stress hormone. And cortisol literally shrinks the part of your brain responsible for motivation. The more you compare, the less motivation you have to actually improve.

[Mini-payoff - 0:40]
So comparison is a catch-22. You compare because you want to improve. But comparing makes it harder to improve. The act of comparison kills the thing you're comparing to get.

[Counter-intuitive - 0:50]
The fix? Don't compare outcomes. Compare systems. Instead of asking "where do I rank?", ask "what did I do yesterday that moved me forward?" Same data, zero comparison.

[CTA - 1:02]
You don't need to be ahead. You need to be in motion. That's the whole game.

## EPISODE A2: "Why motivation fades and what to use instead"

### Layer 1 — Hook
Title (60 chars): Motivation isn't the engine — it's the exhaust
Opening 15s: "Motivation isn't the engine. It's the exhaust. The thing you're waiting for — that feeling of being ready — never comes. Here's why you don't need it."
Implicit promise: Replace motivation with a system that works without it
Retention risk: Title is counterintuitive — must deliver concrete alternative fast

### Short Script (60-90s):

[Hook - 0:00]
Motivation isn't the engine. It's the exhaust.

[Stakes - 0:07]
The feeling of being ready? It never comes. It can't. Your brain is wired to prioritize comfort over action. That's not a character flaw — it's a 200,000-year-old survival mechanism that hasn't updated its firmware.

[Open loop - 0:18]
But here's what the people who actually get things done know — and the rest of us miss.

[Content - 0:25]
Motivation follows action. Not the other way around. Every study on behavioral activation shows the same thing: you don't need to feel ready to start. You need to start to feel ready. Action generates motivation. Waiting for motivation generates nothing.

[Mini-payoff - 0:38]
The two-minute rule. Pick something that takes less than two minutes. Do it right now. Not when you feel like it. Your brain will produce the motivation after you've already moved.

[CTA - 0:50]
You don't need discipline. You need a timer set to two minutes and the willingness to be mediocre for sixty seconds. That's the whole secret.

## EPISODE B1: "The backfire effect"

### Layer 1 — Hook
Title (60 chars): Trying to change someone's mind makes them believe harder
Opening 15s: "When you try to change someone's mind with facts, their brain does the opposite of what you expect. It doubles down. This is the backfire effect, and understanding it changes how you argue forever."
Implicit promise: A counterintuitive mind hack that makes you better at influencing people
Retention risk: Political arguments trigger it — keep examples neutral and relatable

### Short Script (60-90s):

[Hook - 0:00]
When you try to change someone's mind with facts...
their brain does the opposite of what you expect.
It doubles down.

[Stakes - 0:10]
This is called the backfire effect. In 2006, political scientists showed people corrections to false political beliefs. The people who disagreed most... believed the false information MORE strongly after seeing the correction.

[Open loop - 0:22]
Why does evidence backfire? Because your brain treats a challenged belief as a physical threat.

[Content - 0:30]
fMRI studies show that when your core beliefs are challenged, the same brain regions light up as when you're physically under attack. Your amygdala activates. Your prefrontal cortex partially shuts down. You quite literally cannot think clearly when your identity feels threatened.

[Mini-payoff - 0:42]
The fix is counterintuitive. Don't lead with facts. Lead with safety. Say "I used to think the same thing" before you share evidence. Your brain stops defending and starts listening.

[CTA - 0:55]
The goal isn't to win the argument. The goal is to make it safe to change your mind. For them... and for you.

Scene 2: "Brain scan heat map showing red activation in threat regions, medical documentary style"

Scene 4: "Two silhouettes — one rigid with red amygdala glow, one relaxed with blue prefrontal glow"
Scene 5: "Person letting down a defensive shield, warm light entering, transformation scene"


## EPISODE B2: "Why you remember the bad more than the good"

### Layer 1 — Hook
Title (60 chars): Your brain remembers the bad stuff on purpose
Opening 15s: "You remember every embarrassing thing you've said since 2012 but forget compliments from yesterday. That's not bad luck. Your brain is designed to do this. And knowing why changes how you see yourself."
Implicit promise: Understand negativity bias and how to override it

### Short Script (60-90s):

[Hook - 0:00]
You remember every embarrassing thing you've said since 2012.
But you forgot three compliments from yesterday.

[Stakes - 0:08]
That's not bad luck. Your brain is wired to remember negative experiences more vividly than positive ones. It's called negativity bias, and it was useful when remembering which bush hid the tiger was a survival skill.

[Open loop - 0:18]
But here's the real problem.

[Content - 0:25]
Your brain doesn't distinguish between "tiger in the bushes" and "awkward comment in a meeting." Both get stored with high emotional priority. So your memory of the day is shaped more by the one negative moment than by the seven neutral or positive ones.

[Mini-payoff - 0:38]
The fix is simple and research-backed. Write down three good things that happened today. Every day. After 21 days, your brain starts scanning for positive information automatically. You quite literally rewire your attention.

[CTA - 0:52]
Your memory isn't broken. It's optimized for a world that no longer exists. Update the software.
